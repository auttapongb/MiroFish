# Scenario — Thailand's Entertainment Complex / Casino Legalization: The Public's Lingering Questions

## Background
Thailand has edged toward casino legalization through an "Entertainment Complex" bill — a package that
would allow integrated resorts containing a casino (capped at a small fraction of each complex's floor
space), sold to the public as tourism and investment, not gambling. The debate cuts cleanly down the
middle of Thai society, and the public is deeply unsettled, not settled.

In March 2025, NIDA Poll — the country's most-cited opinion research centre — released a nationwide
survey (18–20 March 2025, n=1,310, 18+, telephone interviews, 97% confidence) titled "คำถามคาใจเรื่อง
สถานบันเทิงครบวงจร" — the public's *lingering questions* about the entertainment complex. The results
show a population that is not clearly for or against, but full of unanswered, often anxious questions.

## What the public is asking (NIDA Poll, real figures)
When asked what worries them most about the policy, respondents (multiple answers) ranked:

1. **32.6%** — Will this fill the country with vice (อบายมุข) and destroy national security?
2. **30.2%** — Will it actually improve the economy, and how?
3. **28.1%** — What measures will prevent Thais from becoming gambling addicts?
4. **24.9%** — What measures will prevent money laundering?
5. **24.7%** — How will it be kept from becoming a source of "influential figures" and crime?
6. **20.2%** — What measures exist to prevent social harm?
7. **18.6%** — Will it actually solve illegal gambling dens?
8. **18.6%** — Will it really attract more tourists?
9. **17.5%** — What will ordinary Thai people get from this?
10. **16.6%** — Will it become a funding source for politicians?
11. **12.9%** — Will it create more jobs for Thais?
12. **12.1%** — How will licensing be kept transparent and corruption-free?
13. **12.0%** — Why not hold a referendum first?
14. **10.2%** — Has the government even done a feasibility study?
15. **14.3%** — No lingering questions at all.

And when asked whether the policy will spark social/political conflict, respondents said:

- **31.8%** — a new and *severe* round of conflict,
- **31.7%** — a new round of conflict, but *not* severe,
- **26.5%** — merely disagreement, not conflict,
- **9.2%** — no conflict or disagreement at all.

In other words: nearly two-thirds of the public expects some form of conflict, and almost a third
expects it to be severe. The country is bracing for a fight nobody has yet resolved.

## The trigger
Three pressures collide in one quarter:

1. **The government pushes forward.** Proponents — the business wing and pro-investment camp — argue the
   country is already losing gambling revenue to Singapore and Macau, that underground dens flourish
   anyway, and that a legal, taxed, regulated resort would capture the money and fund infrastructure.
   They frame it as "tourism and investment, not a casino."

2. **The opposition hardens.** The conservative, religious, and moral camp — led by Buddhist voices and
   the anti-casino parties — calls it a moral and social poison, warns of addiction and crime, and
   demands the bill be killed outright rather than regulated.

3. **The public's questions go unanswered.** The NIDA poll lands and dominates the news cycle: the
   public is not convinced by either side because *nobody has answered their actual questions* — about
   addiction, money laundering, crime, transparency, and what ordinary people get. The middle is wide
   open, and both camps are racing to claim it.

## The fault lines
- **Pro-casino** — argue gambling already happens underground, so legalize, tax, and regulate it; the
  resort is "tourism," and it will bring jobs, tourists, and investment that the country needs.
- **Anti-casino** — call it a moral and social poison; fear addiction, crime, money laundering, and the
  erosion of national values and security.
- **The conditional middle** — the largest and most decisive group. They are not ideologically opposed,
  but demand answers first: fair compensation, transparent and corruption-free licensing, anti-addiction
  safeguards, anti-money-laundering rules, and a clear statement of what ordinary citizens get. Win
  this group and the bill passes; lose it, and it dies.
- **The process question** — a meaningful minority (12%) wants a referendum before any decision, and
  many more distrust that licensing will be clean.

## What to simulate
> Simulate the next 12 months of public debate over the Entertainment Complex / casino legalization.
> Does the bill pass, get killed, or get stuck in limbo? Which camp wins the conditional middle — the
> pro-casino promise of jobs and investment, or the anti-casino warning of vice and crime? Who answers
> the public's questions first and most convincingly, and does the answer sway the undecided? Does the
> conflict materialize the way two-thirds of the public expects it to — and if so, is it the severe kind
> (31.8%) or just loud disagreement (26.5%)? Where does the money and the moral argument land, and do
> ordinary Thais — the farmers, vendors, labourers, and grandmothers — end up better off or worse?
